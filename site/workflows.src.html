How the work gets done

Your people already know how the fund works. That has never been the problem.

The problem is the eighteen months it takes to get what they know into a system — through requirement documents, a vendor's configuration language, and consultants who learn your business on your time and then leave with it.

Ratio's premise is that the expert should be able to say it, and the system should do the work of making it precise — then argue back about everything they left out.

What an implementation costs today

Knowledge goes in as prose and comes out as somebody else's config

The usual path

Expensive, slow, and lossy at every step

  • Workshops produce a requirements document, written by someone who does not do the job, read by someone who does not know the fund.
  • Consultants translate it into the vendor's configuration language, which nobody at your firm can read.
  • The translation loses the edge cases — the ones your controller would have mentioned if anyone had asked the right question.
  • You discover which ones in user acceptance testing, or in production, or in an audit.
  • The knowledge ends up in a consultant's head and a config export. When your controller retires, so does the reasoning.

What has actually changed

The translation step is now cheap

  • Turning careful domain prose into a precise draft specification is exactly what a language model is good at.
  • It is also exactly the step that used to cost the most, take the longest, and lose the most detail.
  • The reason nobody could safely automate it before is that a wrong draft is worse than no draft — and nothing could tell you it was wrong.
  • Ratio can tell you. Every draft is compiled against the chart of accounts, the epoch calendar, the type system and the balancing rule, and what does not hold up comes back as a question.
  • So the expert's time goes into answering good questions rather than filling in forms.

The asset was always the expertise. Twenty years of knowing why this fund accrues the way it does is not something a platform can supply, and it is not something that should live in one person's head or in a spreadsheet called fees_v4_FINAL.xlsx. The point of what follows is to get it out of both and into something versioned.

The loop

Say it, draft it, check it, answer back, approve

It is a loop rather than a pipeline, and the return path — the questions coming back — is where most of the value is. A specification nobody interrogated is how implementations go wrong.

Worked example

A genuinely hard one: cross-border dividends with treaty reclaims

An easy example would prove nothing. So: a Luxembourg-domiciled fund holding European equities, where each country withholds tax at source at its own rate, a treaty reduces that rate, the difference is reclaimable years later, and whether you accrue the reclaim depends on the vehicle.

This is the kind of thing that takes a quarter to configure and still produces an audit finding. Here it is, end to end.

Stage one

The brain dump

Recorded in a working session. Note that it is not organized, not complete, and doubles back on itself — which is how expertise actually sounds.

“Right, dividends. French name — France takes 25% at source. But we're a Lux SICAV so under the treaty it should be 15, and we file for the difference. Reclaims take two, three years though, so whether we accrue it depends on the fund: for the UCITS we accrue the full reclaim because that route's been reliable, for the segregated accounts we don't accrue at all until cash lands, the client doesn't want the volatility. We book the receivable gross on ex-date, FX at the ex-date rate, then true up at payment. And keep the reclaimable bit as its own receivable — don't net it against the withholding expense, the auditors want to see both. Germany's different, they're 26.375 with the solidarity surcharge…” Head of fund accounting, session one
Stage two

What the model pulls out

Before drafting anything it separates the facts, the events, the policies and the things that were mentioned but not specified.

extractiondraft
— jurisdictions with two rates each (statutory, treaty)
    FR  25%    → 15%   when fund domicile LU
    DE  26.375% → 15%  incl. solidarity surcharge

— events
    ex_date        book gross receivable, FX at ex_date
    pay_date       true up FX
    reclaim_filed  (mentioned, not specified)
    reclaim_paid   (mentioned, not specified)

— policy that varies BY VEHICLE, not globally
    UCITS       accrue reclaim at 100%
    segregated  accrue at 0% until cash received

— explicit constraint from the expert
    reclaim receivable must NOT net against
    withholding expense — auditors want both visible

The last line matters: it was said in passing, and it is about to catch an error.

Stage three

The first draft

Typed, declarative, and — as it turns out — wrong in one place.

rules/dividends_eu.ratiodraft 1
jurisdiction FR { statutory 25.0% ; treaty 15.0% when domicile = LU }
jurisdiction DE { statutory 26.375% ; treaty 15.0% when domicile = LU }

rule dividend_accrual {
  when   ex_date(security s, gross g, currency c)
  fx     rate(c → fund.base, on: ex_date)
  posts  debit  asset.dividend_receivable   g
         credit income.dividend             g
}

rule withholding_at_source {
  when   ex_date(security s, gross g, ...)
  amount g × jurisdiction(s.issuer_country).statutory
  posts  debit  expense.withholding_tax     amount
         credit asset.dividend_receivable   amount
}

rule treaty_reclaim {
  when   withholding_at_source and treaty_eligible(s, fund)
  amount g × (statutory − treaty)
  accrue fund.policy.reclaim_accrual
  posts  debit  asset.reclaim_receivable    amount
         credit expense.withholding_tax     amount
}
Stage four

What the checks find

Some obligations are discharged mechanically. The rest come back as questions — including one where the draft contradicts something the expert actually said.

  • Every posting nets to zero on every dimension.
  • All five accounts exist, and their normal balances match the directions used.
  • The FX rate resolves to a recorded fact with provenance at ex_date, not a live lookup.
  • Rates are typed percentages; 26.375% is exact, not a float.
  • The draft contradicts your stated constraint. You said the reclaim receivable must not net against withholding expense, but credit expense.withholding_tax does exactly that. Should it credit a separate contra account — expense.withholding_tax_reclaimable — so both stay visible?
  • fund.policy.reclaim_accrual is per-vehicle, and only two vehicles were described. What is the default for a new fund, and who is allowed to set it?
  • Is Germany's solidarity surcharge reclaimable under the treaty, or only the 25% base? The draft assumes the whole 26.375% is in scope, which may over-accrue the receivable.
  • If ex_date and pay_date fall in different accounting epochs, the FX true-up crosses a closed period. Book the difference in the later epoch, or restate?
  • Which FX source, and what happens when it has no rate for that date because the currency's market was closed?

Look at what just happened. The first question is the model catching its own error — not because it reconsidered, but because the expert's own constraint was captured as a checkable obligation and the draft violated it. The other four are things the expert knows perfectly well and did not think to say, because nobody asked. In a conventional implementation each one is discovered later: in testing, in an audit, or in a restatement.

Stage five

Answers, and the corrected rule

Ten minutes of the expert's time, on questions only they can answer.

rules/dividends_eu.ratiodraft 2
rule treaty_reclaim {
  when   withholding_at_source and treaty_eligible(s, fund)
  amount g × (reclaimable_rate(s.issuer_country) − treaty)
  accrue fund.policy.reclaim_accrual   default 0%, set by controller
  posts  debit  asset.reclaim_receivable            amount
         credit expense.withholding_tax_reclaimable amount
}

— DE: surcharge is not treaty-reclaimable; base only
jurisdiction DE {
  statutory        26.375%
  reclaimable_base 25.0%
  treaty           15.0% when domicile = LU
}

— FX true-up crossing an epoch boundary posts to the
— later epoch; closed periods are never restated
rule fx_trueup { on pay_date ; epoch current ; ... }

All eight checks pass. The Germany correction alone would have over-stated the reclaim receivable on every German dividend the fund ever received.

Elapsed: two working sessions and a review. Not because anything was skipped — eight obligations were discharged and five genuine ambiguities were resolved, which is more rigour than the requirements document would have carried. It is faster because the expert spent their time being interrogated about what they know, instead of watching somebody else type it into a form.

Withholding rates are illustrative of the shape of the problem, not tax advice, and treaty positions differ by fund, by security and by year. The point is the process, not the percentages.

Who does what

Nobody is trusting the model with anything

The division of labor is the whole safety argument. The model is the only participant that can be wrong without consequence, because it is the only one whose output is checked by everything downstream.

Responsibility Expert Model Compiler Reviewer
Knows how this fund actually works ●○
Turns words into a precise draft ●
Checks the draft against the chart, the calendar, the types ●
Proves every posting balances ●
Surfaces what was left unsaid ○●
Answers the questions ●
Decides the rule is right ○●
Can cause a number to change ●

● owns it ○ contributes blank — cannot, by construction

The model appears in exactly two rows, both of them drafting. It cannot approve, it cannot post, and it cannot cause a number to move. If it hallucinates an entire tax regime, the compiler rejects the accounts that do not exist, the balancing check rejects the postings that do not net, and a human declines to approve what is left.

What this changes

Expertise stops being a person and starts being an asset

Your knowledge, not a vendor's template

Nobody has to decide which of the vendor's pre-built fee models is closest to how your fund actually works. The rule is what your controller said, made precise — including the parts that are unusual because your fund is unusual.

It outlives the person who knew it

Every rule carries the session it came from, the questions that were asked, the answers given and who approved them. When your controller retires, the reasoning stays — which is not true of a spreadsheet or a consultant.

Small teams stop being disqualified

Complex cross-border treatment has been the preserve of firms that could afford a long implementation. The cost of getting expertise into a system was the barrier, not the expertise itself — and a boutique administrator often has more of the latter than the client they are losing business to.

Changing your mind is cheap

A rule change is a session, a diff and a review — and it can be replayed against last quarter before anyone approves it. Compare that with a change request against a vendor's implementation backlog.

Onboarding a fund is a conversation

The same loop that authored the rules is how a new mandate gets set up: describe how it differs, let the checks find what that implies, and approve. The unusual fund is no longer the expensive one.

The honest caveat. None of this removes the need for someone who genuinely understands fund accounting — it makes that person more valuable, not less. What it removes is the tax that has always been charged on turning their knowledge into a working system.

Say it once. Have it argue back. Approve it.

The expertise was never the bottleneck. Getting it in was.
